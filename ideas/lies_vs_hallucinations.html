Idea 3 of 5

Detect different lies

The literature already proposes several distinctions between types of false outputs (e.g., hallucination, confabulation, strategic deception). However, current lie-detection methods often evaluate detectors on datasets that do not cleanly separate these mechanisms, which makes it difficult to know what the detector is actually detecting. When LLMs produce false statements, how often are these due to epistemic failure versus incentive-driven misreporting?

Concretely: for one model and matched questions, separate false outputs where the model does not know the answer (hallucination) from false outputs where it knows the answer but misreports it under an incentive (lie). What share of false statements falls in each category, and do standard white-box lie detectors and hallucination detectors tell the two apart, or do they respond to falsehood or uncertainty in general?

Background given to every method

Why it matters. White-box deception probes are a leading proposal for monitoring models. If a "lie detector" fires on falsehood in general, or on low-knowledge states, rather than on a mismatch between belief and statement, it will flag honest mistakes and may miss confident knowing lies.

What is known. MASK (2503.03750) is a behavioural benchmark that separates honesty (statement vs elicited belief) from accuracy (belief vs ground truth) using pressure prompts and neutral belief elicitation, without probe analysis of the two error types. Liars' Bench (2511.16035) collects lie and honest examples from four open models (24B-72B) over seven settings and finds detectors fail on some lie types; its focus is lies vs honest answers, not lies vs honest errors. Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2502.03407) train linear deception probes on Llama-3.3-70B with high AUROC and note the probes also fire on deception-related but honest text. Other recent work finds that different deception types have partly distinct representations (2602.01425), that truth probes miss deception without false statements (2603.10003), asymmetric transfer between spontaneous and instructed deception probes (2609.00180), and that hidden states reflect knowledge recall rather than truthfulness, with some hallucinations looking internally like correct answers (2510.09033).

Gap. The area is crowded on "lie type A versus lie type B". Less covered, as far as abstracts show, is a matched, same-question, same-model design that crosses knowledge (the model knows or does not know, checked by neutral elicitation) with incentive (neutral or pressure), and then asks what fraction of false outputs falls where and whether deception probes and hallucination probes confuse the cases. Position the work against the papers above.

Scope and pitfalls. - Models: gated repos (google/gemma-*, meta-llama/*) need the HF_TOKEN environment variable to have been granted access; if a download is refused, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct works without it. Liars' Bench (Cadenza-Labs/liars-bench) is likewise gated on the Hub. - Small models may lie only when instructed. With 7-9B models, incentive-driven lying without an explicit instruction may be rare, so the "lie" category can collapse into instructed or role-play lies, which is the case prior work already criticises. Report the yield of genuine belief-contradicting answers honestly. - Prompt confound: lies always come with a pressure prompt, so a probe can learn to detect the prompt rather than the lie. The comparison is only informative with honest answers given under the same pressure, and with a check on what a probe reads from the prompt tokens alone. - The informative contrast is lie versus hallucination (both false), not lie versus honest-correct. - Belief labelling is noisy: "knows" versus "does not know" from sampling consistency is fuzzy, and a confidently wrong belief is neither a hallucination nor a lie in the usual sense.
