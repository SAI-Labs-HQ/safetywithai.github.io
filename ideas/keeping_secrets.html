Idea 2 of 5

LLMs are bad at keeping obvious secrets

LLMs are bad at keeping obvious secrets. For instance, when LLMs write fiction they foreshadow plot way too much because planning and prose generation are entangled: the model can't help but hint at what it knows is coming. The model wants to say what it's thinking, without being told it has to. Is this still true when it can rely on a plan it's conditioning on that sets up the future so it doesn't have to constantly get ready for that future on its own?

Concretely: when a model writes a story while holding a secret it was told not to reveal, does giving it an explicit plan or outline to follow reduce how much of the secret leaks into the text? And what is the mechanism of the leak: is the secret concept active in the model's internal state throughout generation, and does removing it remove the leakage?

Background given to every method

What is known. The idea's author has since published the behavioural core: Holtzman and West, "Can You Keep a Secret? Involuntary Information Leakage in Language Model Writing" (arXiv 2605.10794, May 2026). A writer model gets a secret word plus an instruction not to mention or hint at it, and writes a story of about 450 words; a guesser model then picks which of two texts was written with the secret (two-alternative forced choice). They report 70-79% discrimination for several frontier models (two were below chance), that an "actively hide" instruction pushes leakage below chance, that short jokes do not leak, and that a decoy word redirects the leakage. That work is behavioural and API-based, and the paper itself says a more mechanistic study is needed. It used 15 secret words: umbrella, lighthouse, violin, cactus, telescope, justice, patience, entropy, nostalgia, freedom, bracket, Tuesday, copper, margin, invoice. Related lines: semantic leakage (Gonen et al. 2025); Taboo model organisms where a secret word is fine-tuned in and recovered with logit lens and SAEs (Cywinski et al.), which does not address involuntary leakage into unrelated text; secrecy without private state (Baldelli et al.); ironic negation (Mann et al.); and evidence that models plan future tokens in hidden states (2502.06258, 2404.00859, and Anthropic's planning-in-poems result).

Gap. Two things appear untouched. (1) The idea's actual question: does conditioning on an explicit plan or outline reduce leakage, and does it hold for plot-level secrets (a twist the model knows is coming) rather than only single words? (2) Mechanism: is the secret represented in the residual stream throughout story generation, does its strength predict leakage, and does ablating it remove leakage? A run that only replicates the published secret-word effect adds little.

Scope and pitfalls. - Model-size threshold (the main risk). The prior paper reports NO leakage for Llama 3.2-3B and Llama 3.1-8B, marginal leakage for Gemma 3 4B (59%), and strong leakage (83-84%) for Gemma 3 12B/27B and Llama 3.3 70B. A writer model of about 8-9B or smaller is therefore expected to show a floor effect with nothing to explain. Gemma 3 12B is the smallest model reported to leak strongly. - Models: gated repos (google/gemma-*, meta-llama/*) need the HF_TOKEN environment variable to have been granted access; if a download is refused, unsloth/gemma-3-12b-it works without it. SAEs for this model are in google/gemma-scope-2-12b-it (ungated; newer and less supported tooling). An OpenRouter API key is available as OPENROUTER_KEY (for example for the guesser model). - Confound in any plan condition: an outline both adds context (which may just dilute the secret's salience) and fixes content decisions. A comparison is only informative if it can tell these apart, for example against length-matched irrelevant context. - The known effects give yardsticks: the plain "secret plus do not reveal" condition is a positive anchor, and the decoy word is a known mitigation to compare effect sizes against. - Measurement: guesser judgments are noisy and position-biased, so both presentation orders and enough trials are needed to separate, say, 80% from 65%. For plot-level secrets, candidate twists may be guessable from the premise alone, so a no-secret control is needed. For ablations, compare against random and other-concept directions and check that story quality is not simply degraded.
